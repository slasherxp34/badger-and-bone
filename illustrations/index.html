About the illustrations

How the dogs are built, what the drawing system is, and why they are drawings and not photographs.

The short version: every dog on this site was created for Badger & Bone and for nobody else — original drawings from our own six-coat system, authored as code rather than licensed, traced or generated as an image. There is no stock illustration, no clip art, no traced artwork, and nothing from an image generator. The dogs are parametric vector drawings — built as code in our own drawing system, with AI-assisted tooling used to write and refine that code, never to produce the image. Six coats, three poses, one drawing system — and the source files are the same ones your mug is printed from.

How they are made

There is no illustrator’s signature to give you, because the dogs are not drawn freehand — they are authored as code. The drawing system is a set of vector primitives and rules that produce every coat and pose; AI-assisted tooling was used to write and refine that code, and every proportion, cue and color below is a rule set deliberately in it.

What we can be specific about is the system, because a system is checkable in a way that a claim about a person is not. Everything below is a rule the files actually follow.

The 3:1 rule

The whole thing starts with one proportion. A dachshund is drawn three times longer nose to tail than it is tall at the shoulder, and that ratio is treated as a specification rather than a joke. It is why the puzzle is a landscape composition and not a tidy square, why the art prints run wide rather than tall, and why the mug gives up having a front at all — the drawing wraps the whole way round, because a dachshund does not fit on one face.

Five breed cues have to survive in every pose, at every size, or the drawing is not finished: a long level back that never arches, legs about a third of the body depth, a low-set floppy ear hanging past the jawline, a tapered wedge of a snout rather than a thin beak, and an upturned tail sweeping up and back. Miss the ear and you have drawn a corgi. Arch the back and you have drawn a whippet.

One continuous body path

The head, neck, torso and rump are a single unbroken outline — not a set of blocks butted together and hoped over. This is the one construction detail that separates a drawn dog from an assembled one, and it is the reason the silhouette still reads when it is printed at an inch and a quarter on an ID tag.

You can check this yourself, which is the point of putting it here. Every dog on this page is inline SVG rather than an image file, so your browser’s inspector shows you the actual drawing. In the standing red dog, the body is one <path> carrying 19 curve and line commands in an unbroken loop from the snout, over the skull, along the back, round the tail, back under the belly and up the chest to where it started. The other 10 paths in that file are the tonal steps, the shading on the underside and the ear — they sit on the body, they are not what makes it. We would rather you checked than took our word for it.

There are no outlines and no gradients anywhere in the system. Shapes are defined by their own edges, and depth comes from exactly three tonal steps of one coat color — far-side legs a shade darker, the body at its base tone, paws a shade lighter. The eye is a single dark circle with one white catchlight, placed forward of the ear so that it does not fill in and go blind at small sizes.

The six coats

Six coats, drawn as six drawings rather than one drawing recolored six times. The coat is the one thing every product on this site has in common, and it is the thing a dachshund owner is actually shopping for — they are not buying a mug, they are buying their dog on a mug.

Two of those coats are texture rather than color, and both are clipped to the body path: dapple’s spots and the wirehaired hatch stop exactly at the silhouette’s edge. A spot that spills past the outline is the single most common giveaway of art that was recolored rather than drawn, and it is the reason cream needs a darker ear — a pale dog on a warm oat background loses its head entirely otherwise.

How a drawing becomes a product

Nothing is redrawn freehand for a one-off. The same source files feed everything, which is what keeps the dog on your ornament the same dog as the one on your mug:

  1. The drawing is authored as vector. Rounded rectangles, circles and arcs — a deliberately small set of primitives, so a new pose is built from the same parts as the last one.
  2. It is scaled in inches, not pixels. A dog runs roughly 2.2–3.6 inches nose to tail depending on the product, so it reads at physical size. Scaling by a fraction of the canvas instead is how you end up with confetti on an 18-inch print.
  3. It is laid out per product, around the object. The mug uses the full wrap, because a dachshund on a cylinder only works if the nose starts near the handle and the tail arrives back where it began. The puzzle runs long rather than square. The sticker is cropped to a silhouette that still reads at three inches.
  4. It goes to a US print partner as print-ready art. We print to order — nothing sits in a warehouse — so the file our partner receives is the file drawn here.

The same artwork also runs the site itself: the patterns behind the section bands, the 404 page, the empty cart. It is one system, used everywhere, which is the only way six coats stay consistent across eleven products.

Why drawings and not photographs

Two honest reasons, in order of honesty.

The first is that we are new. Badger & Bone opened this year, the first samples are on their way, and photographing a product we have not held yet would mean using a supplier’s stock mock-up and calling it ours. Real photographs of real products will go up as soon as those samples arrive, alongside these drawings rather than instead of them.

The second is that an illustration is the only way to promise you your dog. A photograph of one red dachshund is a photograph of one red dachshund. Six drawn coats let a dapple owner buy a dapple. That is a thing a photo shoot could not do at this size, and it is why the drawings would stay even once the camera work exists.

Rights, and who we are not

The art is original and it is ours. Every illustration, pattern and mark on this site was created for Badger & Bone and nobody else. We do not use stock illustration, clip art, traced artwork, licensed characters, or image-generator output in any customer-facing design. Our illustration system is written as code and iterated with AI-assisted tools; the shapes, proportions and colors are ours, defined explicitly in that code rather than sampled from anyone else’s work. Copyright in all of it is retained by Badger & Bone (sole proprietorship, Los Angeles, CA).

We are not affiliated with any kennel club, breed registry or breed association — not the American Kennel Club, not any national or regional breed club, and not any other organization of that kind. Nothing here is endorsed, sanctioned, licensed or approved by one. “Dachshund” and “long dog” are used descriptively, to describe the dog the products are drawn for, and nothing more.

We also hold no affiliation with any other pet or fashion brand that uses a dachshund in its own marks. Our drawings are deliberately distinct from theirs.

You are welcome to share, post and repost these images with credit. Please do not print them onto goods for sale, or use them as your own brand’s artwork. If you want to use one for something — a rescue fundraiser, a club newsletter, a press piece — email us and ask. The answer is usually yes.
